# Breathing & ENT — From First Breaths to Clear Airways

**Quick Summary (60 seconds)**

**Urgency: Important — know the red flags at every age.**

**The short version:** Your child's respiratory and ear-nose-throat health will likely generate more doctor visits than any other system in the first five years. Most of what you encounter — colds, ear infections, croup, snoring — is common, temporary, and manageable. But a few situations require fast action, and knowing the difference can save you both unnecessary worry and dangerous delay.

**What to expect at a glance:**
- **Newborn (0–3 months):** Noisy breathing is usually normal. Sneezing is normal. Learn what respiratory distress actually looks like.
- **Infant (3–12 months):** Bronchiolitis season, first colds, possibly first ear infection. RSV protection is now available.
- **Toddler (1–3 years):** Croup's barky cough arrives. Ear infections peak. Adenoid problems may begin.
- **Preschool (3–5 years):** Adenoid and tonsil issues are most common now. Allergies may emerge. Asthma diagnosis becomes possible.
- **School-age (6–12 years):** Asthma management, allergy testing, fewer ear infections.
- **Teen (13–18 years):** Exercise-induced breathing issues, allergic rhinitis, sinus problems.

**When to seek emergency care:** Retractions (skin pulling in with each breath). Nasal flaring. Blue or gray lips. Stridor at rest. Inability to speak or cry. Drooling with inability to swallow. Any breathing that looks like hard work. Trust your instincts — if your child's breathing frightens you, go.

## Newborn breathing (0–3 months)

Newborns are noisy breathers, and most of the sounds that alarm new parents are completely normal. Your baby's airways are tiny — the diameter of a drinking straw — so every bit of mucus, every slight position change, produces sound that would be inaudible in an adult. [Source: src1]

**What's normal:**
- **Respiratory rate of 30–60 breaths per minute.** That's much faster than yours. Count for a full 60 seconds — newborns breathe irregularly.
- **Periodic breathing.** Pauses of up to 10 seconds between breaths, sometimes with a few rapid breaths afterward, are normal in the first few weeks. This is not apnea — it's an immature breathing pattern that resolves on its own. [Source: src1]
- **Sneezing.** Newborns sneeze frequently, and it almost never means they have a cold. Sneezing is how they clear their tiny nasal passages of mucus, dust, and amniotic fluid remnants.
- **Nasal congestion without illness.** Because newborns are obligate nasal breathers — they breathe almost exclusively through their noses for the first three months — even minor congestion sounds dramatic. Nasal saline drops and gentle bulb suctioning before feeds and sleep are your best tools. [Source: src2]

**What's not normal — signs of respiratory distress:**
- Breathing consistently faster than 60 breaths per minute
- Retractions — you can see the skin pulling in at the neck, between the ribs, or below the ribcage with each breath
- Nasal flaring — nostrils widening with each breath
- Grunting — a sound with each exhale, which is the body's attempt to keep the airways open
- Blue or gray color around the lips or fingernails (cyanosis)
- Pauses in breathing longer than 20 seconds, or shorter pauses accompanied by color change or a drop in heart rate

If you see any of these signs, seek immediate medical attention.

### RSV and bronchiolitis

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is the most common cause of bronchiolitis — an infection that inflames the small airways in the lungs. Nearly all children get RSV by age two, but it's most dangerous in the first six months of life, in premature infants, and in children with heart or lung conditions. [Source: src3]

**What bronchiolitis looks like:** It typically starts as a regular cold — runny nose, mild cough, maybe a low fever. Over two to three days, the cough worsens, and you may hear wheezing. The concern is when breathing becomes labored or feeding becomes difficult because the baby can't breathe and eat at the same time.

**Treatment is supportive.** There is no medication that shortens bronchiolitis. Albuterol (the asthma inhaler medication), steroids, and antibiotics do not help. What does help: keeping the nose clear with saline and suctioning, ensuring adequate hydration, and monitoring breathing. [Source: src4]

**RSV prevention — a major advance.** Until recently, the only RSV prevention was palivizumab (Synagis), limited to high-risk infants. Now there are two new options:

- **Nirsevimab (Beyfortus):** A long-acting monoclonal antibody given as a single injection to infants. Recommended for all infants under eight months entering their first RSV season (typically October through March) if the mother did not receive the RSV vaccine during pregnancy. Dosing: 50 mg for infants under 5 kg, 100 mg for those 5 kg and above. [Source: src5]
- **Abrysvo (maternal RSV vaccine):** Given to the pregnant parent at 32–36 weeks gestation. Protects the newborn through transferred antibodies. Most infants need one approach or the other, not both. [Source: src5]

Talk to your pediatrician or obstetrician about which option is right for your family. This is one of the most significant advances in infant health in recent years — RSV hospitalizations in infants have dropped substantially since these products became available.

## Croup: the 2 AM barking cough

If you've never heard croup before, the sound is genuinely alarming — a harsh, barking cough that sounds like a seal, often accompanied by a hoarse voice and a high-pitched whistling sound (stridor) when your child breathes in. Croup is caused by a viral infection that swells the airway just below the vocal cords. It's most common between six months and three years of age, and it has a particular fondness for showing up in the middle of the night. [Source: src6]

**The good news:** Croup sounds much worse than it usually is. Most cases are mild and resolve within three to seven days. Symptoms are typically worst on the second or third night.

**What helps at home:**
- **Stay calm.** Crying and agitation make stridor worse because they tighten the airway further. Comfort your child.
- **Cool air.** Take your child outside into cool night air for 10–15 minutes, or sit in a bathroom with a cool mist humidifier. Many parents notice the drive to the ER cures the croup — that's the cool air effect.
- **Fluids.** Small, frequent sips.
- **Upright position.** Hold your child upright or prop them up — lying flat worsens symptoms.

**Medical treatment:** A single dose of oral dexamethasone (a steroid) is highly effective for croup at all severity levels. It begins working within two to three hours and its effects last 24 to 48 hours. If your child has croup with stridor at rest, your pediatrician or ER will likely give dexamethasone and may add nebulized epinephrine for faster relief. [Source: src6]

**Go to the ER if:**
- Stridor is present at rest (not just when crying or agitated)
- Your child is drooling or unable to swallow
- Significant retractions — you can see the neck and chest muscles working hard with each breath
- Blue or gray lips
- Your child appears unusually sleepy or agitated
- The stridor is getting worse, not better, over the course of hours

**Epiglottitis — rare but critical.** Before the Hib vaccine, epiglottitis (infection of the flap above the voice box) was a feared emergency. It's now rare but still possible. Unlike croup, epiglottitis causes high fever, drooling, a muffled (not hoarse) voice, and a child who sits very still in a "sniffing" position. If you suspect epiglottitis, call 911 — do not try to look in your child's throat.

## Adenoids and tonsils — the hidden troublemakers

This section deserves real depth because adenoid and tonsil problems are among the most common — and most underdiagnosed — issues in young children. Many parents spend months or years managing symptoms (snoring, mouth breathing, recurrent ear infections, poor sleep) before someone connects the dots.

### What are adenoids?

Adenoids are a pad of lymphoid tissue that sits high in the back of the nose, behind the nasal passages, where you can't see them by looking in your child's mouth. (Tonsils, by contrast, are the two lumps visible at the back of the throat.) Adenoids are part of the immune system and help trap germs entering through the nose — especially important in the first few years of life.

Here's the issue: adenoids grow from birth, reaching their peak size between ages three and seven, which is exactly when a child's airway is still quite small. In many children, the adenoids grow large enough to partially or fully block the nasal airway and the Eustachian tubes (the tiny channels that drain the middle ear). After age seven or eight, adenoids typically begin to shrink, and by puberty they've usually regressed significantly.

### How to recognize adenoid problems

Because you can't see the adenoids, you have to recognize them by their effects. The classic pattern includes several or all of these:

**Breathing signs:**
- **Chronic mouth breathing** — your child breathes through their mouth during the day, not just when congested from a cold. The mouth is open at rest. During sleep, the mouth is always open.
- **Loud snoring** — not the occasional snoring of a stuffy nose, but loud, nightly snoring that you can hear from another room.
- **Sleep apnea** — pauses in breathing during sleep, followed by gasping or snorting. Restless sleep, unusual sleeping positions (hyperextended neck, sleeping sitting up), bedwetting in a previously dry child, and night sweats can all be signs.
- **"Adenoid facies"** — a long face with an open-mouth posture that develops over time in children who chronically mouth-breathe. The facial structure can actually change with prolonged mouth breathing during the years of facial growth.

**Ear signs:**
- **Recurrent ear infections** — the enlarged adenoids physically block the Eustachian tubes, preventing normal drainage. Fluid gets trapped behind the eardrum, creating an environment for bacteria to grow.
- **Persistent fluid behind the eardrums** (otitis media with effusion) — even between acute infections. This can cause muffled hearing and, if prolonged, affect speech and language development.
- **Hearing loss** — often subtle. Your child may turn up the TV volume, say "what?" frequently, or seem inattentive (sometimes mistaken for a behavioral issue).

**Other signs:**
- Hyponasal voice — sounds like they're always stuffed up, even when they don't have a cold.
- Bad breath (halitosis) despite good dental hygiene.
- Chronic runny nose or nasal congestion that doesn't respond to allergy medications.

### When surgery is the right call

Not every child with large adenoids needs surgery. Many children's symptoms are mild enough to manage with watchful waiting, and the adenoids will shrink on their own as the child grows. But surgery is the right choice when the adenoids are causing significant, ongoing problems:

**Strong indications for adenoidectomy:**
- **Obstructive sleep apnea** confirmed by sleep study or clear clinical history — this is the most urgent indication. Children with untreated OSA can develop behavioral problems that mimic ADHD, poor growth, cardiovascular strain, and cognitive difficulties. [Source: src9]
- **Recurrent acute otitis media** — three or more infections in six months, or four or more in twelve months, especially when adenoid hypertrophy is confirmed.
- **Chronic otitis media with effusion** — fluid behind the eardrums persisting for three months or longer, particularly when associated with hearing loss. Adenoidectomy is often combined with ear tube placement in this situation.
- **Chronic sinusitis** that hasn't responded to medical treatment.
- **Significant impact on quality of life** — severe nasal obstruction affecting eating, sleeping, or daily functioning.

**What to expect from surgery:**

Adenoidectomy is one of the most common pediatric surgeries and one of the safest. It's typically an outpatient procedure — your child goes home the same day. Recovery takes about a week, with mild throat discomfort and possible bad breath during healing. [Source: src10]

The outcomes are excellent. Studies show adenoidectomy improves sleep apnea symptoms in approximately 85 percent of children. It does not weaken the immune system — by the time the adenoids are causing enough problems to warrant removal, the rest of the immune system has matured enough to compensate fully. The complication rate is very low: primary bleeding occurs in about 0.3 percent of cases, and serious adverse events are rare. [Source: src10]

If your child also has enlarged tonsils contributing to airway obstruction, the surgeon may recommend removing both adenoids and tonsils (adenotonsillectomy). Recovery is somewhat longer for tonsillectomy — typically 7 to 14 days — and throat pain is more significant, but the combined procedure addresses both sources of obstruction at once.

**After surgery:** Children with severe obstructive sleep apnea may need a follow-up sleep study to confirm that the obstruction has resolved. For most children, parents notice dramatic improvement within the first few weeks — quieter sleep, closed-mouth breathing, fewer ear infections, better daytime energy and behavior.

## Ear infections

Ear infections are the single most common reason children are prescribed antibiotics. About 80 percent of children will have at least one acute ear infection by age five, with the peak incidence between six and 24 months. If it feels like your toddler has a permanent ear infection, you're not alone. [Source: src7]

### Why young children get so many ear infections

Anatomy. A young child's Eustachian tube — the channel connecting the middle ear to the back of the throat — is shorter, narrower, and more horizontal than an adult's. This means fluid doesn't drain well, and germs from the throat can reach the middle ear more easily. As the child grows and the skull matures, the Eustachian tube becomes longer and more angled, which is why ear infections become less common after age five or six.

Additional risk factors include daycare attendance (exposure to more viruses), bottle-feeding while lying flat, pacifier use after six months, secondhand smoke exposure, and a family history of ear infections.

### Acute ear infections vs. fluid behind the eardrum

These are different conditions with different management:

**Acute otitis media (AOM)** is a bacterial infection of the middle ear. Your child may have ear pain (tugging at the ear in babies who can't tell you), fever, irritability, difficulty sleeping, and sometimes drainage from the ear. The eardrum looks red and bulging when examined.

**Otitis media with effusion (OME)** is fluid behind the eardrum without active infection. It often follows an acute ear infection and can persist for weeks or months. The child may not have pain or fever, but the fluid causes muffled hearing — like hearing underwater. This is why a child who seems inattentive or isn't meeting speech milestones should have their ears checked.

### When antibiotics are needed — and when they're not

About 70 percent of acute ear infections resolve on their own within two to three days. The AAP guidelines support a watch-and-wait approach for certain situations: [Source: src7]

**Watch and wait (48–72 hours with pain management) is appropriate for:**
- Children 24 months and older with mild symptoms and a one-sided infection
- Children 6–23 months with mild symptoms and a one-sided infection (without drainage)
- The key criteria: ear pain for less than 48 hours, fever below 102.2 degrees Fahrenheit (39 degrees Celsius), and pain manageable with acetaminophen or ibuprofen

**Antibiotics right away for:**
- All children under 6 months
- Severe symptoms — moderate to severe ear pain, ear pain lasting 48 hours or more, fever of 102.2 degrees Fahrenheit or higher
- Both ears infected in children 6–23 months
- Ear drainage (ruptured eardrum)

When antibiotics are prescribed, amoxicillin remains the first-line treatment. Finish the full course as prescribed.

### Ear tubes

If your child has recurrent ear infections — three or more in six months, or four or more in a year — or if fluid behind the eardrums persists for more than three months with associated hearing loss, your doctor may recommend tympanostomy tubes (ear tubes). [Source: src8]

Ear tubes are tiny cylinders placed through a small incision in the eardrum during a brief outpatient procedure under general anesthesia. They allow fluid to drain from the middle ear, equalize pressure, and reduce the frequency of infections. The tubes typically fall out on their own in six to eighteen months as the eardrum heals.

This is one of the most common pediatric surgeries — roughly 667,000 are performed annually in the United States. The procedure takes about 10 to 15 minutes, and most children are back to normal activities the next day.

## Asthma

Asthma is the most common chronic disease of childhood, affecting about 8 percent of children in the United States — roughly six million kids. It causes recurring episodes of wheezing, coughing, chest tightness, and shortness of breath when the airways become inflamed and narrowed. With proper management, the vast majority of children with asthma can live fully active lives, including playing sports. [Source: src11]

### The diagnosis challenge in young children

Here's the frustrating reality: asthma is difficult to diagnose definitively in children under five or six, because they can't reliably perform the lung function tests (spirometry) that confirm the diagnosis. Many young children wheeze when they have colds — about 30 percent of children will wheeze before age three — and most of them will not develop asthma. [Source: src12]

So how do doctors decide? They use a combination of symptom patterns, risk factors, family history, and response to treatment. The Modified Asthma Predictive Index helps identify which wheezing young children are more likely to develop asthma. The risk is higher with recurrent wheezing plus one major factor (parent with asthma, child has eczema, or allergic sensitization to airborne allergens) or two minor factors (allergic rhinitis, wheezing without colds, or blood eosinophils at 4 percent or above). [Source: src12]

If your child under five has recurrent wheezing, your pediatrician may suggest a trial of asthma medication. If the symptoms improve with treatment, that supports (but doesn't prove) an asthma diagnosis. The formal diagnosis often comes later when lung function testing is possible.

### Triggers

Understanding and minimizing your child's triggers is as important as medication:

- **Viral infections** — the most common asthma trigger in young children. You can't avoid all colds, but good hand hygiene and keeping your child home when sick helps.
- **Allergens** — dust mites, pet dander, mold, pollen, cockroach allergens. Environmental triggers account for up to 80 percent of asthma flares in urban children. [Source: src11]
- **Cold air and exercise** — common triggers, especially together. A warm-up period before exercise and pre-treatment with a rescue inhaler can help.
- **Tobacco smoke** — one of the strongest modifiable risk factors. If anyone in the household smokes, quitting is the single most impactful thing they can do for a child with asthma.
- **Air pollution, strong odors, cleaning products.**

### Treatment: the step-wise approach

Asthma treatment follows a step-wise approach, adjusted up or down based on how well symptoms are controlled:

- **Quick-relief (rescue) inhaler:** Albuterol (salbutamol) — used as needed for acute symptoms. Every child with asthma should have one. For young children, use a metered-dose inhaler with a spacer and facemask, not a nebulizer, unless the child can't cooperate with the spacer. [Source: src13]
- **Daily controller medication:** Low-dose inhaled corticosteroids (such as budesonide or fluticasone) are the first-line daily medication for persistent asthma. These reduce inflammation and prevent flares. They are not the same as anabolic steroids — at standard doses, they are safe for long-term use in children.
- **Step up if needed:** If low-dose inhaled steroids aren't enough, the next step may be increasing the dose or adding a long-acting bronchodilator (LABA). Children requiring step 3 or above should see a pediatric pulmonologist or allergist.

### The asthma action plan

Every child with asthma should have a written asthma action plan, developed with their doctor and shared with schools and caregivers. It uses a traffic-light system:

- **Green zone (doing well):** No symptoms, normal activity. Continue daily controller medications.
- **Yellow zone (caution):** Coughing, mild wheezing, waking at night, or symptoms with exercise. Use rescue inhaler, possibly increase controller. Follow the plan's specific instructions.
- **Red zone (emergency):** Rescue inhaler not helping, severe shortness of breath, difficulty speaking in full sentences, lips blue or gray. Use rescue inhaler and seek emergency care immediately.

## Allergies and the nose

Allergic rhinitis — commonly called hay fever, though it has nothing to do with hay or fever — is one of the most common chronic conditions in children. Prevalence rises with age: over 5 percent at age three, about 8.5 percent by ages six to seven, and nearly 15 percent by adolescence. [Source: src14]

### How to tell allergies from a cold

Both cause sneezing, runny nose, and congestion. Here's how to tell them apart:

| | Allergic rhinitis | Common cold |
|---|---|---|
| **Duration** | Weeks to months (as long as exposure continues) | 7–10 days |
| **Nasal discharge** | Clear and watery | Starts clear, may become thick/yellow-green |
| **Itching** | Nose, eyes, throat, ears — prominent | Minimal |
| **Fever** | Never | Possible |
| **Pattern** | Seasonal pattern or triggered by specific exposures | Random, more common in fall/winter |
| **Other clues** | "Allergic salute" (rubbing nose upward), "allergic shiners" (dark circles under eyes), horizontal crease across nose | Sore throat, body aches |

### Allergy testing

If your child's symptoms are significantly affecting their quality of life — chronic congestion, poor sleep, missed school — allergy testing can identify specific triggers. There is no strict minimum age for testing. Skin prick testing has a sensitivity of 68 to 100 percent and can be performed at any age, though it's most commonly done starting around age two to three. Blood tests (serum-specific IgE) are an alternative that works at any age and doesn't require stopping antihistamines. [Source: src14]

### Immunotherapy: the only disease-modifying treatment

While antihistamines and nasal corticosteroid sprays manage symptoms, allergen immunotherapy is the only treatment that can actually change the underlying immune response. It involves gradually exposing the body to increasing amounts of the allergen, training the immune system to tolerate it. [Source: src15]

Immunotherapy comes in two forms: allergy shots (subcutaneous, given in a doctor's office) and sublingual tablets or drops (taken under the tongue at home). Treatment typically lasts three to five years. Real-world data shows it reduces medication use, asthma flares, and the need for oral steroids over nine years. It may also prevent the progression from allergic rhinitis to asthma — the so-called "allergic march." Immunotherapy is generally started at age five and older, though it can be considered earlier in specific cases.

## Common colds — the unavoidable reality

Young children get sick a lot. This is normal, expected, and — in a developmental sense — necessary. Their immune systems are encountering viruses for the first time, building the library of immune memory that will serve them for life. [Source: src16]

**How many colds to expect:**
- **Infants and preschoolers:** 6 to 8 per year (up to 10 to 12 in daycare settings)
- **School-age:** 5 to 6 per year
- **Adolescents:** about 4 per year

That means a toddler in daycare can seem perpetually sick from September through April. Each cold lasts 7 to 10 days, and with overlapping infections, it can feel like one continuous illness. This is immunologically normal, not a sign of a weak immune system.

### What actually helps

- **Nasal saline irrigation** — evidence supports using saline washes multiple times daily to speed resolution and reduce the need for other medications. [Source: src16]
- **Honey** (age one and older) — has evidence supporting its use for cough, and children generally accept it. Do not give honey to children under one year due to botulism risk.
- **Pain and fever relief** — acetaminophen or ibuprofen (ibuprofen only for children six months and older).
- **Fluids and rest.**
- **Humidifier** in the bedroom.

**What does not help:** Over-the-counter cough and cold medicines are not recommended for children under six. They have not been shown to be effective in young children and carry risks of side effects. And antibiotics do not treat colds — colds are caused by viruses, and giving antibiotics for a viral infection does nothing except cause side effects (diarrhea, rashes) and contribute to antibiotic resistance. [Source: src16]

### When a cold isn't just a cold

See your doctor if:
- Fever lasts more than three days, or goes away and comes back
- Ear pain or drainage from the ear
- Symptoms lasting more than 10 days without improvement (suggests possible bacterial sinusitis)
- Rapid or labored breathing, wheezing
- High fever — above 104 degrees Fahrenheit (40 degrees Celsius) at any point in a child of any age
- Your child looks significantly worse, not just "cold-sick" — unusually sleepy, refusing all fluids, persistent vomiting

## Sleep-disordered breathing — more than just snoring

About 7 to 12 percent of children snore habitually, and most of the time it's harmless. But 2 to 4 percent of children have obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) — a condition where the airway partially or completely collapses during sleep, causing repeated interruptions in breathing. The peak age is two to six years, and the most common cause is enlarged adenoids and tonsils. [Source: src9]

### Why it matters beyond sleep

Here's what makes pediatric OSA particularly important: it can masquerade as behavioral problems. Children with untreated sleep apnea don't typically present as sleepy during the day the way adults do. Instead, they become hyperactive, inattentive, and emotionally dysregulated — symptoms that overlap significantly with ADHD.

The numbers are striking: studies show that 25 to 57 percent of children with ADHD have sleep-disordered breathing, and in research using overnight sleep studies, 50 percent of children with ADHD were found to have obstructive sleep apnea. Multiple studies have demonstrated that treating OSA — usually through adenotonsillectomy — improves ADHD symptoms. The AAP recommends screening children with hyperactive or inattentive behavior for sleep-disordered breathing. [Source: src9]

Other consequences of untreated OSA in children include poor growth, bedwetting, cardiovascular strain, and cognitive difficulties including problems with memory and executive function.

### Signs to watch for

- Loud snoring most nights — not just when congested
- Pauses in breathing during sleep, followed by gasps or snorts
- Restless sleep, frequent position changes, unusual sleeping positions
- Mouth breathing during sleep
- Night sweats, bedwetting (in a previously dry child)
- Daytime: behavioral problems, difficulty concentrating, hyperactivity, morning headaches, irritability

### Diagnosis and treatment

The gold standard for diagnosing OSA is an overnight polysomnography (sleep study). This measures airflow, oxygen levels, brain activity, muscle activity, and heart rate during sleep. Your pediatrician may refer you for a sleep study if your child has habitual snoring along with any of the signs listed above.

**Treatment:**
- **First-line: Adenotonsillectomy.** Cures OSA in approximately 85 percent of otherwise healthy children. The improvement is often dramatic — parents frequently report that their child seems like a different person after surgery.
- **If surgery isn't appropriate or doesn't fully resolve OSA:** CPAP (continuous positive airway pressure) delivered through a mask during sleep.
- **Adjunct treatments:** Weight management if obesity is a contributing factor. Nasal corticosteroid sprays and montelukast can help with mild residual OSA after surgery.

## Choking and foreign body aspiration

Choking is the fourth leading cause of accidental death in children and the third leading cause in infants under one year. The highest risk period is ages one to three, when children are mobile enough to find small objects, developmentally driven to put everything in their mouths, and don't yet have the molars or coordination to chew food safely. Eighty-one percent of foreign body aspiration cases occur in children under three. [Source: src17]

### Dangerous objects

**Food** (most common category):
- Peanuts and tree nuts — the most frequently aspirated food item
- Hot dogs — the most fatal food item (17 percent of food aspiration deaths), due to their cylindrical shape that perfectly seals a child's airway
- Whole grapes, cherry tomatoes, large blueberries — round shape is the danger
- Popcorn, raw carrots, hard candy, seeds

**Non-food:**
- Latex balloons — the most fatal non-food item (29 percent of aspiration deaths)
- Small toy parts, coins, button batteries, pen caps, small magnets

### Prevention

- **Cut food into small, manageable pieces.** Grapes and cherry tomatoes should be quartered lengthwise (not just halved). Hot dogs should be quartered lengthwise, then sliced.
- **No hard, round, or sticky foods before age four:** whole nuts, popcorn, hard candy, chunks of raw carrot or apple, large spoonfuls of peanut butter.
- **Supervise all meals and snacks** — no eating while running, walking, playing, or riding in a car.
- **Keep small objects out of reach.** Check under furniture and between couch cushions. Follow toy age recommendations.
- **No latex balloons for young children.** Mylar balloons are safer.

### What to do if a child is choking

**If the child can cough forcefully:** Encourage them to keep coughing. Do not intervene — a strong cough is the most effective way to clear the airway.

**If the child cannot cough, cry, or breathe:**
- **Infant (under 1 year):** Place face-down on your forearm, give 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades, then turn face-up and give 5 chest compressions. Repeat until the object is expelled or the infant becomes unresponsive. Do NOT use abdominal thrusts on an infant.
- **Child (over 1 year):** Stand behind the child, place your fist just above the navel, and give abdominal thrusts (Heimlich maneuver) until the object is expelled or the child becomes unresponsive.
- **Call 911** if the object doesn't come out, the child becomes unresponsive, or the child is turning blue.

The AAP recommends that all parents, caregivers, and childcare providers learn infant and child CPR and choking rescue techniques. Many hospitals, fire departments, and community organizations offer classes. [Source: src17]

## When to go to the emergency room

Knowing when your child needs emergency care versus a next-day doctor visit can feel impossible in the moment. Here's a practical guide for respiratory and ENT emergencies. [Source: src18]

### Call 911 or go to the ER immediately for:

**Breathing emergencies:**
- **Retractions** — skin visibly pulling in at the neck, between ribs, or below the ribcage with each breath. This means the body is using accessory muscles to breathe — it's working too hard.
- **Nasal flaring** — nostrils widening with each breath
- **Cyanosis** — blue or gray color around the lips, inside the lips, or on the fingernails
- **Grunting** with each exhale
- **Stridor at rest** — a high-pitched sound when breathing in, heard without a stethoscope, while the child is calm
- **Breathing significantly faster** than normal for age
- **Inability to speak, cry, or feed** due to breathing difficulty
- **Tripod position** — the child is leaning forward on their hands, unable to lie down

**Specific emergencies:**
- **Croup with stridor at rest**, drooling, or severe distress — needs epinephrine and steroids
- **Asthma attack not responding to rescue inhaler** — if the inhaler hasn't helped after the action plan's recommended doses, don't wait
- **Suspected epiglottitis** — high fever, drooling, muffled voice, very still child in "sniffing position." Call 911. Do not examine the throat.
- **Anaphylaxis** — sudden wheezing or stridor combined with hives, swelling, or vomiting. Give epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen) if available. Call 911.
- **Choking with complete obstruction** — child cannot cough, cry, or speak. Begin rescue maneuvers. Call 911.

### Normal respiratory rates by age (so you have a reference):

| Age | Normal breaths per minute |
|---|---|
| Newborn | 30–60 |
| Infant (1–12 months) | 30–60 |
| Toddler (1–3 years) | 24–40 |
| Preschool (4–5 years) | 22–34 |
| School-age (6–12 years) | 18–30 |
| Adolescent (13–18 years) | 12–20 |

**When in doubt, go.** No emergency physician will ever fault you for bringing in a child whose breathing worried you. It is always better to be seen and reassured than to wait and wish you hadn't.
